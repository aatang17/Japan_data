# Japan Inc. Spent Sixty Years Buying Each Other's Shares. Now It Is Selling Them and Calling It Profit.

*Introducing the Plover Analytics equity screens — every annual securities report
on EDINET, tagged line by line. 3,809 filers, ¥56tn of cross-shareholdings, and the 168
companies whose profit this year was mostly a one-off.*

---

Tokyo Broadcasting System is a television network. It employs journalists, makes dramas,
sells advertising, and is regulated as a broadcaster.

It also owns **¥1,016bn of investment securities against ¥1,628bn of total assets.**
Sixty-two per cent of the company is a share portfolio.

The largest single line in it is **Tokyo Electron, booked at ¥562.6bn** — very close to
half of TBS's entire net assets, in one stock. TBS's own filing explains why it holds it,
and the explanation is remarkable: Tokyo Electron was *established in 1963 as a
wholly-owned subsidiary of Tokyo Broadcasting System*. The broadcaster incubated a
semiconductor equipment maker during the Kennedy administration and never sold.

This post is about what happens when a company like that finally does sell. It is the
first from the **Plover Analytics equity screens**. Start here:

## Part 1: What TBS actually is

Take the segment disclosure from the FY03/2026 annual report at face value:

| Segment | Revenue | Segment profit | Segment assets |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Media & content | ¥312.2bn | ¥14.6bn | ¥342.4bn |
| Lifestyle | ¥95.7bn | ¥2.9bn | ¥89.4bn |
| **Real estate & other** | **¥16.9bn** | **¥7.3bn** | **¥256.5bn** |

Real estate is **4% of revenue and 29% of segment profit.** That is the "TBS is really a
property company" argument in one line, and as far as it goes it is correct — the Akasaka
land is carried cheap and earns well.

But the property is the smaller half of the story. The rental real estate note discloses
land and buildings **carried at ¥76.8bn and worth ¥361.4bn** — ¥285bn of value that never
appears on the balance sheet. Meanwhile the share portfolio sits *on* the balance sheet at
full market value, and it is nearly three times larger.

Add it up. TBS's disclosed cross-shareholdings run to 38 positions worth **¥897bn — 79% of
net assets.** Tokyo Electron ¥562.6bn, Recruit ¥102.9bn, Sky Perfect JSAT ¥53.3bn, Toho
¥37.7bn, Toei ¥35.8bn. ISS recommends voting against the top executive of any Japanese
company holding 20% or more of net assets in cross-shareholdings. TBS is at four times
that line.

So: not a broadcaster that owns property. A **share portfolio that owns a broadcaster**,
and the broadcasting is the part that employs everybody.

## Part 2: This year, it started selling

In FY03/2026 TBS booked **¥48.9bn of gains on sales of investment securities.** Net of
extraordinary losses, the one-off contribution was **¥44.1bn against pre-tax profit of
¥81.5bn — 54%.**

Now watch what that does to the headline ratio.

- **Reported ROE: 5.1%** — below the market's 8.3% median, because the portfolio inflates
  the denominator as well as the numerator.
- **ROE excluding the one-off: 2.3%.**

Two point three per cent. That is what TBS's actual businesses — the network, the studios,
the Akasaka buildings — returned on the capital shareholders have left in the company. The
portfolio is simultaneously the source of the profit and the reason the equity base is too
big to earn a return on.

There is nothing improper here. The gain is disclosed on the face of the income statement,
exactly where it should be. The problem is downstream: it flows into profit, into ROE, into
every screen that ranks on ROE, and into the story that Japanese corporate returns are
rising.

## Part 3: TBS is not unusual

We ran the same test across every company with an extracted annual report — **3,809
filers, one latest filing each.** The test: net one-off gains (extraordinary income *minus*
extraordinary losses) as a share of pre-tax profit.

**168 companies owe at least 30% of their pre-tax profit to a one-off.** Together those
gains are **¥1,234bn.** Grouped by what the gain actually was:

- **Selling investment securities — 80 companies, ¥572bn.** The cross-shareholding unwind.
- Selling fixed assets — 37 companies, ¥374bn. Mostly land and buildings.
- Negative goodwill — 16 companies, ¥86bn. Buying a business for less than the book value
  of its net assets and booking the difference as income.
- Selling subsidiary shares, business transfers, subsidies — the remainder.

The first line is the one that matters. Japan has spent three years being told that the
governance code is forcing companies to unwind their cross-shareholdings. It is happening.
What nobody says out loud is the accounting consequence: **an unwind is a realised gain,
and a realised gain is profit.** Eighty companies this year earned more from selling their
partners' shares than from a third of everything else they did.

And it does not repeat. Every share sold is a share that cannot be sold again.

## Part 4: The ROE that isn't there

For the 168, strip the one-off out and re-base profit at each company's own effective tax
rate. The median ROE goes from **9.0% to 3.9%.** For **twenty of them it goes negative.**

The extreme cases are worth naming, because they are the ones a screen puts at the top:

- **児玉化学工業 (4222)** reports **ROE of 144.9%** — the second highest on the market. Its
  operating margin is 3.2%. The profit is a **¥22.6bn negative-goodwill gain**. Ex the
  gain, ROE is **13.8%**: a good year, not a record.
- **千趣会 (8165)** reports **ROE of 26.1%** on an **operating loss of ¥2.6bn**. A ¥7.1bn
  property sale carried the year. Ex the sale: **−17.9%.**
- **サン電子 (6736)** reports a **97.5% net margin** on a **−0.2% operating margin.** All of
  the ¥9.7bn profit is equity-method income and a gain on a change in equity interest.

None of these companies has done anything wrong. Every one of them told you, in the filing,
exactly what happened. The failure is on the reading side: a ranking on reported ROE is a
ranking on transactions that happened once.

**An honest caveat.** In aggregate this is not a market-wide mirage: ¥1,234bn of net

concentrated, not systemic. It matters enormously for the 168 companies and for anyone
screening on ratios, and very little for the index.

## What these screens do that a terminal doesn't

**1. They net the losses off.** さくらインターネット booked ¥14.5bn of extraordinary income
against ¥0.24bn of pre-tax profit, which looks like the most extreme case in Japan — until
you read the next line, a **¥14.3bn reduction-entry loss** offsetting the same government
subsidy. It nets to zero. 西武ホールディングス's ¥58bn construction contribution is the
same trick. A screen on gross extraordinary income flags both; this one doesn't, because
gross extraordinary income is not a number that means anything.

**2. Every figure is the filed figure, and the calculation is shown.** The yen amounts here
are tagged lines from each company's 有価証券報告書 on EDINET — not recomputed, not
adjusted. The ratios *are* calculated, so they carry their formulas instead of a badge:
ROE is profit attributable to owners over the average of opening and closing equity, and
the adjustment is reported ROE × (pre-tax profit − net one-off) ÷ pre-tax profit. Filers
disagree with each other on ROE conventions — 児玉化学 prints 82.8% on closing equity where
the consistent method gives 144.9% — so we show ours, theirs, and the gap.

**3. Every view has a permanent URL.** Every link in this post is a live page, not a
screenshot, with the underlying data one button away as CSV.

A missing value is shown as `—`. Never as zero.

## What I'll be watching

**Who is left to sell.** 2,458 companies disclose a cross-shareholding portfolio, worth
**¥56.1tn** at book. **265 of them hold 20% or more of net assets** in other companies'
shares — the ISS threshold. Kyoto Financial Group is at 85% of net assets, TBS at 79%,
Kyocera and MS&AD at 47%. That is the pipeline of future "earnings", and it is
knowable in advance.

**Whether it repeats.** This screen sees one year per company. A firm that has sold
shareholdings every year for a decade is running a policy; one that did it once is dressing
up a bad year. The filings hold five years of history and separating the two is the next
piece of work.

**The denominator.** TBS earns 2.3% on equity ex the gain — not because the business is
uniquely bad, but because a trillion yen of other companies' shares sits in its equity. Sell
the portfolio, buy back stock, and ROE doubles without a single thing changing in the
business. Several companies are going to discover this, and the market is going to reward
it. Whether it should is a different question.

*(One blind spot, stated plainly: the 297 IFRS filers in the universe have no 特別利益
section, so a one-off gain sits inside operating or other income where this test cannot see
it. The screen is Japan GAAP only.)*
